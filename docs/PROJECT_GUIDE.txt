PART 1 — WHAT THE PROJECT IS ABOUT
================================================================================

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1.1  THE ONE-PARAGRAPH VERSION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

When an AI model is published, the author signs it so that anyone downloading it
can prove two things: who made it, and that nobody altered it. Today that
signature uses RSA or ECDSA. A sufficiently large quantum computer running
Shor's algorithm breaks both. Our project replaces that signature with a
quantum-safe one (NIST FIPS 204 ML-DSA-65), enforces it inside a blockchain's
own transaction rules so a bad signature physically cannot be recorded, and
hashes the model layer-by-layer so that if someone tampers with it we can say
exactly which layer was changed.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1.2  THE CORE INSIGHT — "THE IMMUTABILITY PARADOX"
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

This is the idea that makes the project interesting, and the one to lead with
when presenting.

A blockchain is valuable because it is append-only: history cannot be rewritten.
A blockchain also stores signatures and public keys permanently and publicly.

Put those two facts together with a quantum computer and you get a trap:

  Step 1 (today, 2026)  Acme Corp signs "model v1" with ECDSA. The signature and
                        the public key are written to the ledger forever, visible
                        to everyone, replicated across thousands of nodes.

  Step 2 (2035-2040)    A Cryptographically Relevant Quantum Computer (CRQC)
                        exists. An attacker reads Acme's public key straight off
                        the public ledger — no hacking required, it was always
                        public — and uses Shor's algorithm to derive the matching
                        private key.

  Step 3 (2035-2040)    The attacker signs a backdoored model with that recovered
                        key and back-dates the provenance. The record looks like
                        it was created in 2026, before the break.

  Step 4                Nobody can fix it. Rewriting ledger history would break
                        consensus. The forged entry is now as permanent as the
                        genuine ones.

So the property that makes a blockchain trustworthy — immutability — is exactly
what makes the damage permanent and unrepairable. Every signature written today
is a liability that matures when quantum computing arrives.

KEY PRESENTATION LINE:
"Every ECDSA signature on a public ledger today is a time bomb. The blockchain
 will faithfully preserve the forgery forever."


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1.3  TWO DIFFERENT ATTACKS THAT PEOPLE CONSTANTLY CONFUSE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The paper is careful about this, and examiners often probe it.

  (a) HNDL — "Harvest Now, Decrypt Later"       → a CONFIDENTIALITY attack
      The attacker records encrypted traffic today and decrypts it years later
      once the key exchange is broken. It targets one conversation at a time.
      DEFENCE: ML-KEM-768 (FIPS 203) to wrap the AES-256-GCM content key
               instead of ECDH.

  (b) RETROSPECTIVE FORGERY                     → an INTEGRITY attack
      The attacker recovers a private key from a published verification key and
      mints signatures that appear historical. Not limited to one target —
      it poisons the entire trust chain backwards in time.
      DEFENCE: ML-DSA-65 (FIPS 204) signatures.

These are different attacks needing different primitives. A signature scheme
does nothing for confidentiality; a KEM does nothing for forgery. The framework
addresses both.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1.4  WHY AI MODELS SPECIFICALLY, AND NOT JUST "FILES"
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

AI artefacts have three properties that break normal provenance tooling:

  1. THEY ARE HUGE.  A 7 GB model cannot be put on-chain. Gas costs alone would
     be astronomical. So the artefact lives off-chain (IPFS) and only a
     fixed-size commitment goes on-chain.

  2. THEY ARE DERIVED CONSTANTLY.  Fine-tunes and LoRA adapters change a handful
     of matrices out of hundreds. Re-hashing and re-signing the entire 7 GB file
     for a 40 MB change is wasteful.

  3. THEY ARE A DESERIALISATION ATTACK SURFACE.  The most common real-world
     supply-chain attack on AI today is NOT quantum — it is arbitrary code
     execution through Python pickle when you call torch.load(). If the code
     runs during loading, a signature you check afterwards is worthless.

Our answers, in order: content-addressed off-chain storage; a per-tensor Merkle
tree so derivatives re-sign only changed leaves; and an SDK that refuses
pickle-backed formats outright, accepting only safetensors and ONNX.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1.5  THE FOUR OBJECTIVES (from the paper, Section 1.4)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  O1  INTEGRATED DESIGN
      Compose FIPS 204 ML-DSA-65, a Fabric/Ethereum dual-layer ledger and IPFS
      content addressing into one deployable architecture.

  O2  ENFORCED, NOT ADVERTISED  ←── the most important one
      Verify the post-quantum signature as an ENDORSEMENT CONDITION inside
      Fabric chaincode, so an invalid signature cannot be committed at all.
      Most prior work merely stores the public key on-chain and leaves
      verification optional and client-side. Ours makes it structural.

  O3  AI-SPECIFIC TAMPER LOCALISATION
      Replace flat whole-file hashing with a per-tensor Merkle tree, enabling
      single-layer tamper identification, streaming verification without full
      download, and cheap delta-signing of derivatives.

  O4  HONEST RESIDUAL-RISK ACCOUNTING
      Document the boundary of the quantum guarantee instead of overstating it,
      including the secp256k1 dependency of the Ethereum anchor transaction.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1.6  SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE — SEVEN COMPONENTS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Flow is "publish-then-verify":

  [1] Signer CLI          ML-DSA-65 + Ed25519 composite signing
  [2] Merkle Hasher       per-tensor SHA-256 leaves → binary Merkle tree
  [3] IPFS (Kubo)         content-addressed storage, CIDv1 dag-pb
  [4] Fabric Gateway      gRPC transaction submission
  [5] Fabric Chaincode    *** VERIFY — ENFORCED *** ← the critical component
  [6] Anchor Contract     Ethereum; stores commitments ONLY, never verifies
  [7] Consumer SDK        re-verifies on fetch; refuses to return a model object
                          if any check fails

CRITICAL POINT FOR THE VIVA:
Verification happens at [5], not only at [7]. That is the whole thesis.
  - Fabric ENFORCES: an invalid signature is rejected during endorsement and
    never enters the ledger. Cost: ~0.3 ms in Go with the CIRCL library, and
    Fabric has no gas metering, so the check is free.
  - Ethereum RECORDS: verifying a 3,309-byte lattice signature in EVM opcodes
    would be prohibitively expensive, so the contract stores five fixed-size
    bytes32 commitments and nothing more.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1.7  THE CRYPTOGRAPHY, PRECISELY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

CHOSEN PARAMETER SET: ML-DSA-65, NIST Security Category 3
(comparable to AES-192 key search, roughly 2^192 classical effort)

  Parameter Set   Level        Public Key   Private Key   Signature   Round-3 sig
  ML-DSA-44       Category 2   1,312 B      2,560 B       2,420 B     2,420 B
  ML-DSA-65  ←    Category 3   1,952 B      4,032 B       3,309 B     3,293 B
  ML-DSA-87       Category 5   2,592 B      4,896 B       4,627 B     4,595 B

NOTE: the last column is the SUPERSEDED Round-3 Dilithium value. Many published
surveys still quote those older numbers. We use the final FIPS 204 figures. If
an examiner says "I thought Dilithium-3 signatures were 3,293 bytes", that is
the Round-3 number — FIPS 204 standardised 3,309.

SECURITY BASIS: Module-LWE and Module-SIS — lattice problems for which no
polynomial-time quantum algorithm is known. Signing uses Fiat-Shamir-with-aborts
rejection sampling (Lyubashevsky's trapdoor-free construction).

COMPOSITE SIGNATURES:
  A signature is Ed25519 ‖ ML-DSA-65 concatenated; the verifier requires BOTH to
  be valid. During the migration window this hedges against an unforeseen break
  in lattice cryptanalysis. Cost: exactly +64 bytes. Total envelope 3,373 B.

DOMAIN-SEPARATED CONTEXT BINDING:
  context  = "QRB-v1" ‖ chainID ‖ modelId ‖ version ‖ pkHash
  leaf_i   = SHA-256( name_i ‖ dtype_i ‖ shape_i ‖ tensor_bytes_i )
  root     = MerkleRoot( sort_by_name( leaf_0 … leaf_n ) )
  H_meta   = SHA-256( model_card ‖ dataset_hash ‖ eval_metrics )
  signed_μ = SHAKE256( context ‖ root ‖ H_meta )

  Because chainID and version are inside the signed digest, a signature valid
  for v1 on Fabric is worthless for v2 or on Sepolia. This is what defeats
  cross-version and cross-chain replay, and the fault-injection page proves it.

  NOTE ON THE REPORT: Section 3.2 and Section 3.3 state μ slightly differently.
  We unified them into the single formula above. Worth stating one way in the
  final report.

CONFIDENTIALITY PATH:
  Weights encrypted with AES-256-GCM; the content-encryption key is wrapped with
  ML-KEM-768 (FIPS 203) rather than ECDH. ML-KEM-768 public key is 1,184 B and
  the shared secret is 32 B, which becomes the AES-256 key directly.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1.8  PER-TENSOR MERKLE HASHING — WHY IT MATTERS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A flat SHA-256 over a serialised model file gives you ONE BIT of information:
matched, or did not match. It also requires downloading all 7 GB before
verification can even begin.

Parsing the safetensors container and hashing each named tensor separately, then
sorting canonically by name and folding into a binary Merkle tree, buys three
properties a flat hash simply cannot provide:

  TAMPER LOCALISATION   A failed inclusion proof names the exact tensor —
                        e.g. "layer.2.attn.q_proj.weight" — not just "the file
                        changed".

  STREAMING VERIFICATION Check each tensor as its bytes arrive and abort at the
                        first mismatch. In our demo this aborts after ~180 KB of
                        a 790 KB artefact; at production scale that is the
                        difference between 200 MB and 7 GB of wasted transfer.

  DELTA PROVENANCE      A LoRA adapter re-signs only the changed leaves and
                        records a pointer to its parent root, producing a
                        lineage DAG instead of a flat list of unrelated files.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1.9  THE HONEST LIMITATION (objective O4) — DO NOT HIDE THIS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The Ethereum anchoring transaction is authorised by msg.sender and is therefore
signed with secp256k1, which is NOT quantum-safe.

A quantum adversary who recovers a publisher's externally-owned-account key can
write anchor records. They CANNOT forge the ML-DSA signature those anchors
reference, so the forged anchors fail verification — but they can still be
committed to the chain.

  Correct framing: this is a DENIAL-OF-SERVICE and CONFUSION risk,
                   NOT a forgery-of-provenance risk.

Rated MEDIUM in the STRIDE table, deliberately not argued down to LOW.
Second MEDIUM risk: unpinned IPFS content is garbage-collected. Content
addressing guarantees integrity but NOT persistence — hence three pinning nodes
plus a Filecoin storage deal.

Presenting this honestly is a strength, not a weakness. Examiners respect a
project that states its own boundary.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.7  TWO BUGS FOUND AND FIXED DURING IMPLEMENTATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Worth mentioning if asked "what was hard":

  1. SAFETENSORS ALIGNMENT. Creating a Float32Array view over the data segment
     threw a RangeError because the segment did not start on a 4-byte boundary.
     Real safetensors producers pad the JSON header with trailing whitespace to
     force 8-byte alignment. We now do the same.

  2. AMBIGUOUS μ DEFINITION IN THE PAPER. Section 3.2 and Section 3.3 give
     different formulations of the signed digest. We unified them. The final
     report should state it one way.

    - EVIDENCE TO CAPTURE: CIDv1 values and pin status output

3.2  PHASE 2 — THE SIGNER CLI AND CONSUMER SDK
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  SIGNER CLI
    - liboqs / python-oqs for keypair generation and composite signing
    - PyNaCl for the Ed25519 half
    - Validate the per-tensor Merkle hasher against a genuine 1 GB model

  CONSUMER SDK (Python package)
    - fetch by CID, stream-verify tensors, check ML-DSA signature, confirm

    - MUST refuse to return a model object if any check fails

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3.3  PHASE 3 — SCALE VALIDATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  - Benchmark per-tensor Merkle construction at 10 MB, 100 MB, 1 GB and 7 GB
    against flat SHA-256 (we currently test up to ~1 MB in-browser)
  - Fabric endorsement latency and TPS with signature verification enabled
    versus disabled, to isolate the true cost of enforcement
  - Amortised gas cost per model when N state transitions are batched under a
    single Merkle root
  - Replace all browser-JS timings with Go CIRCL timings (expect roughly 0.3 ms
    per verification, far faster than our JS upper bound)

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3.4  PHASE 4 — CLOSING THE SECP256K1 GAP  (the main research direction)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

This is the answer to the one MEDIUM risk we cannot currently mitigate.

  OPTION A — ERC-4337 SMART ACCOUNT
    Replace the externally-owned account with a smart contract account carrying
    an ML-DSA verifier module, so the anchor transaction itself is authorised
    post-quantum rather than by a secp256k1 signature.

  OPTION B — zk-SNARK VERIFICATION
    Prove correct off-chain lattice verification and have the EVM check only a
    succinct proof, avoiding the prohibitive cost of running ML-DSA in EVM
    opcodes directly.

Both are identified in the paper as future work. Neither is claimed as
implemented — say so plainly if asked.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4.8  LIKELY QUESTIONS AND HOW TO ANSWER THEM
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q: "Is the cryptography real or simulated?"
A: "The cryptography is real — ML-DSA-65, Ed25519, SHAKE256, ML-KEM-768 and
    AES-256-GCM all genuinely execute in the browser via the noble libraries.
    The Fabric peers, the Solidity contract and the IPFS daemon are simulated in
    JavaScript, but they reproduce the paper's decision logic exactly, and the
    signatures they check are genuine. When you saw that corrupted signature
    rejected, that was a real lattice verification failure."

Q: "Why ML-DSA-65 and not 87?"
A: "Category 3 gives roughly AES-192-equivalent security, which is the standard
    recommendation for long-lived data. ML-DSA-87 would add about 1,300 bytes
    per signature for security we don't need at this threat level. The
    architecture is parameter-agnostic — swapping to 87 is a one-line change."

Q: "Why two ledgers? Isn't that over-engineering?"
A: "They do different jobs. Fabric is permissioned, has no gas metering, and can
    therefore afford to verify a 3,309-byte lattice signature on every
    transaction — that's the enforcement layer. Ethereum gives public,
    tamper-evident proof of existence that doesn't depend on trusting the Fabric
    consortium. Doing the signature check on Ethereum would be prohibitively
    expensive; doing public anchoring on Fabric is impossible because it's
    permissioned."

Q: "Why not just put the model on-chain?"
A: "A 7 GB model at Ethereum's storage costs is astronomically expensive — our
    benchmark shows that even a 1,952-byte public key costs about 1.24 million
    gas. So the artefact goes to content-addressed storage and only fixed-size
    commitments go on-chain."

Q: "What's the performance penalty?"
A: "Two separate costs. The signature is about 52× larger than Ed25519 — that's
    unavoidable with lattice cryptography. Verification in Go with CIRCL is
    about 0.3 ms, which is negligible inside Fabric endorsement since there's no
    gas metering. The per-tensor Merkle overhead versus a flat hash is shown
    live on the Benchmarks page."

Q: "Isn't the quantum threat overhyped / decades away?"
A: "Possibly — but that's precisely why this matters now. The attack is
    retrospective. A signature written today sits on an immutable ledger waiting
    to be broken in 2035. You cannot retrofit the protection later, because you
    cannot rewrite the chain. NIST finalised FIPS 204 in August 2024 specifically
    because migration has to start before the threat materialises."

Q: "What's the weakest part of your system?"
A: "The secp256k1-signed anchor transaction, and we say so in the report. A
    quantum adversary could write forged anchors — they'd fail verification but
    they'd still be committed. It's a DoS and confusion risk rather than a
    forgery risk. ERC-4337 or zk-SNARKs close it, and both are future work."

Q: "Why does pickle matter? This is a quantum project."
A: "Because the most common real supply-chain attack on AI today isn't quantum
    at all — it's arbitrary code execution through torch.load's pickle
    deserialisation. If the code runs while loading, a signature you verify
    afterwards is worthless. So the SDK refuses pickle-backed formats outright
    and accepts only safetensors and ONNX. It'd be incoherent to solve a 2035
    threat while leaving the 2026 one open."

Q: "How is this different from just signing with Dilithium on a blockchain?"
A: "That composition is already anticipated in the literature. Our contribution
    is the harder problem: enforcing post-quantum authorship inside a
    permissioned ledger's endorsement policy, at a payload scale where the
    artefact cannot go on-chain, with verification granularity matched to the
    internal structure of neural network files. It's the per-tensor Merkle tree
    and the chaincode-level enforcement that are novel, not the use of ML-DSA."


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4.9  THINGS TO AVOID SAYING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  DON'T say "quantum-proof" or "unbreakable". Say "quantum-resistant" or
  "post-quantum". No one claims lattice problems are unconditionally hard.

  DON'T claim the whole system is quantum-safe. The anchor transaction isn't,
  and an examiner who spots that after you've overclaimed will not be kind.

  DON'T call the Fabric/Ethereum/IPFS layers "implemented" without qualifying
  that they're simulated in the browser. Be precise — it costs nothing and
  protects your credibility.

  DON'T quote the Round-3 Dilithium signature sizes. Use FIPS 204's values.

  DON'T say the browser benchmark numbers are the system's real performance.
  They're a JavaScript upper bound; Go CIRCL is far faster.
